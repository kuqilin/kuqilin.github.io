Shenzhen junior high students are steeped in internet trash talk and profanity. Frontline teachers have limited disciplinary powers, and some parents are uncooperative or even file complaints, leaving …

After teaching for several years at an above-average middle school in Shenzhen, Teacher Guiyi has discovered that the “prestigious university halo” she carried upon graduation is becoming less and less effective in front of today’s junior high students.

This young teacher, a graduate of a top-985 university, never imagined that while she stands at the podium each day teaching students to be cultured and polite, the moment break time comes, her students trot out a full arsenal of trash talk and profanity learned from short videos — completely erasing whatever civility they absorbed in class.

“Apart from trash talk and curse words, today’s students are practically losing the ability to speak properly." Guiyi sighs with resignation.

This sense of helplessness is common among young frontline teachers in Shenzhen.

As is well known, Shenzhen places great emphasis on education. Schools there attract large numbers of master’s and doctoral graduates from 985, 211, and even top overseas universities with decent salaries and permanent positions. But this cohort of passionate educators entering basic education is now receiving a cold dose of reality.

01 “Psychological Bombardment” at the Desk: Shenzhen Teachers Surrounded by Trash Talk

Teacher Guiyi’s desk is separated from the classroom by just one wall. What should have been a seat that brings her closer to her students has become the greatest source of agony in her workday.

“During working hours, I’m subjected to a nonstop bombardment of students’ trash talk and profanity. It’s truly painful." Guiyi says that every break time, the classroom erupts like a boiling pot — there are always students shouting all kinds of internet slang at the top of their lungs.

Today’s junior high students have a clear linguistic divide.

When chatting among peers, the coarseness often makes teachers furrow their brows: “They can’t go three sentences without dropping words like ‘SB’ or ‘CNM.’ The most common things you hear are ‘Why are you being so Tang’ or ‘You’re making a fuss over nothing.’”

Guiyi has noticed that students often say these words with the playful smirk of roughhousing. Their homeroom teacher once seriously explained in a class meeting that “Tang” (referring to Down syndrome) is a medical condition, and using it to mock others is a form of secondary harm to patients — it shouldn’t be used casually.

“At the time, everyone nodded in agreement after hearing this. But as soon as break time came and they saw a classmate answer a question wrong, they’d still tease them by saying, ‘You’re acting so Tang.’”

Guiyi feels helpless. “It has become their mantra. It requires no thought, no processing — they just blurt it out in all sorts of situations.”

Xiaohongshu user @教育界在逃史官 has observed that trash talk disrupts classroom discipline. When a student gives a less-than-perfect answer in class, other students often interject loudly without permission, shouting, “You’re such a noob.”

The observer notes that this kind of linguistic phenomenon has occurred in all eras and at all schools, but now it’s appearing frequently even in Shenzhen primary schools, and the density of trash talk has reached new heights.

When facing teachers, however, their vocabulary shifts — less crude, but still saturated with internet catchphrases.

“When speaking to teachers, ‘666,’ ‘What’s the big deal,’ ‘So what,’ ‘You do it if you’re so good,’ ‘Just deal with it’ — you can’t go three sentences without these.” This indifferent, dismissive, and defiant attitude can ignite an adult’s temper in just a few words, leaving teachers with the frustrating feeling of punching a cotton ball.

What hurts frontline teachers even more is the coldness behind this trash talk — it makes one feel that some students’ capacity for empathy has been mercilessly devoured by the internet.

Teacher Muyue, who teaches at another school in Shenzhen, once gave a life education class. At the time, there had just been a major fire in Hong Kong, and she had specially prepared materials, hoping to guide students toward empathy.

When she displayed the fire scene on the big screen and solemnly asked, “After seeing this news about the fire, how do you all feel?”

The students below did not quiet down. Instead, they laughed and shouted, “So what?”

Muyue suppressed her emotions and continued, “What does this person make you think of?”

Several boys in the back row burst out laughing: “Curry Curry!” (a basketball meme)

“Then what would you like to say to these disaster victims?”

“Just deal with it.”

Standing at the podium and looking at these Shenzhen children from privileged backgrounds, Muyue could hardly believe her ears. She couldn’t help but press further: “What if something like this happened to your family, to you — would you still answer the same way?”

The students below remained utterly unperturbed and giggled out two words: “For sure.”

“In other classes, I put up with them saying ’noob,’ ‘yabba dabba doo,’ ‘whoever speaks is gay,’ ‘shine my shoes.’” Muyue says, “But in a life education class, in the face of life and disaster, I can’t accept this kind of indifferent attitude.”

In the eyes of teachers, when childhood is completely occupied by short-form, instant-gratification “digital pacifiers” like short videos and game livestreams, what children lose is not just civilized language, but also the ability for complete logical reasoning and deep thinking.

“This is a kind of cultural aphasia,” Teacher Guiyi analyzes. Now at the end of term, students suddenly started madly spreading the phrase ‘Shaoyu is awesome.’ Whatever they see — funny, impressive — they use these four words as a substitute. Just like the previous ‘amazing amazing,’ it has become their unified response to everything.

“This is a form of intellectual laziness. They don’t want to express their views more precisely, don’t want to use their brains for deep thinking, so they’ve gotten used to crudely generalizing everything with a catch-all internet meme.”

This is directly reflected in learning outcomes. In some exams, when encountering difficult questions they can’t answer on the answer sheet, students simply write internet trash talk on it.

When writing essays or doing reading comprehension, students are unwilling to carefully craft their words, and their ability to precisely express feelings and moods has visibly degraded.

More subtly, those students in the class who don’t participate in meme culture or don’t know the latest internet slang have instead become the outsiders.

Classmates who do know the memes will mock them: “You don’t even know that? How out-of-touch are you? So Tang!”

To fit into the so-called collective and avoid being excluded, students who originally didn’t know have no choice but to force themselves to learn and use it — thus creating an ever-worsening spectacle of universal trash talk.

02 Direct Confrontation: The 5-Yuan “Class Rule” and Shrinking Disciplinary Powers

Faced with this pervasive linguistic environment of trash talk, teachers, fed up as they are, have not stood idly by.

Teacher Guiyi established strict class rules: “We agreed at the time that whoever openly said vulgar trash talk in class or in private, once caught, would have to pay 5 yuan into the class fund; if it was especially bad, 10 or 20 yuan.”

Every time a fine was levied, Guiyi would have the student sign a slip of paper stating the reason. At the end of term, she would chip in some of her own money and use the fund to collectively buy gifts and prizes for the children, stating which students had also contributed.

This method was indeed effective at first, and the children were willing to accept it. But over time, Guiyi noticed something was off.

“This only manages the surface, not the mindset. It can’t bring about any fundamental change. They’re just avoiding fines, not genuinely believing they’ve done something wrong.”

The children began to develop a sense of luck. When they thought the teacher wasn’t in the office or wasn’t paying attention, they would still freely spout vulgar trash talk among classmates.

And once caught by the teacher, they would just stick out their tongues, not seeing it as a problem, thinking instead that today was just bad luck — they hadn’t hidden it well enough.

Muyue once gave her students a psychology class themed “Adventures in the ‘Just Deal With It’ Kingdom.” Since students loved to use “just deal with it” as a catchphrase, Muyue tried to make them imagine themselves in others’ shoes, experience what it feels like to be told “just deal with it,” and come up with coping strategies.

“After the class, most students were indeed moved and said they wouldn’t say it anymore. But it was only that one phrase. Other memes just kept coming in an endless stream.”

What makes teachers feel even more powerless is that the disciplinary tools at their disposal are dwindling.

The “fine-based class rule” that had persisted for several months was quickly shut down. School leadership privately sought out Guiyi, explaining that times have changed — once a student or parent files a complaint, the teacher is in the wrong on all counts.

Guiyi fell silent. If fines are not allowed, what about traditional punishment methods?

“Teachers today basically have no real disciplinary authority left.” Guiyi smiles bitterly. Even if you want to assign copying as punishment, the rules say it must be limited to 500 characters.

“For a junior high student, 500 characters of copying doesn’t serve as any meaningful deterrent. In their minds, teachers no longer have any real authority.”

This “empty-handed” confrontation leaves many top-985 teachers often sinking into deep self-doubt in the office.

“Every time I hear students say these trash memes, I feel deeply disappointed in my own teaching — a strong sense of defeat.” Guiyi says, “Why is it that in class, in life, we keep teaching students positive, uplifting things, and yet the influence from the internet is so much faster — it can completely wash away years of sustained education by the teacher?”

Much of the time, teachers feel like they’re waging a futile war against the entire online environment.

“We are powerless. We simply can’t fight against this phenomenon. All we can do now is to do our best, to try to keep them from turning the campus into a marketplace — to keep it from becoming so dirty, so chaotic.” Guiyi says.

03 Uncooperative Parents: An Inescapable Educational Deadlock

In contemporary education, if the school is the dam, then family is the most solid support behind it.

Teacher Guiyi and her colleagues often say in private discussions: “Even if a student has the weakest foundation and the worst habits, as long as the parents are willing to cooperate, teachers have the confidence and the passion to guide them to shine.”

However, in Shenzhen — a first-tier city with an extremely high awareness of rights and an extremely fast pace of life — the degree of family cooperation is becoming the last straw that crushes teachers’ motivation.

“Students are hard to deal with, but some parents are often even harder to deal with.”

Whenever a homeroom teacher discovers that a child is swearing constantly at school and filling their homework with trash memes, and tries to communicate with parents via WeChat or phone calls to seek cooperation, what comes back is often not understanding, but defensiveness and even hostility.

The types of parents Guiyi encounters fall into three main categories:

The first type unconditionally stands on the child’s side.

These parents themselves don’t have particularly high educational attainment and are easily influenced by certain online videos, believing that “when conflicts arise between teachers and students, parents must unwaveringly defend their child.”

“They feel that when they were young, they were suppressed by teachers, and their own parents didn’t stand up to protect them. Now that they are adults themselves, they must unconditionally back their child up.” Guiyi says. In their eyes, a teacher reporting that their child is using vulgar trash talk at school is an act of harming the child.

The second type seeks to shift responsibility through confrontation with the teacher.

These parents have also tried hard at home to manage their child, having erupted into countless conflicts over phone use and trash talk.

When they receive a call from the teacher, the parent’s sense of defeat transforms into a self-comforting defense.

Guiyi analyzes that these parents feel: I’ve already put in so much effort. The teacher is negating my efforts, trying to shift the school’s educational responsibility. If the child is still like this at school, then the teacher hasn’t done their job properly.

The third type — more common in Shenzhen — are the “highly educated parents” who trust their own judgment more.

Many Shenzhen parents hold high academic degrees and high-ranking positions, and are very confident in their own educational methods.

When a young teacher from a 985 university speaks with them, they often disagree with the teacher’s views, thinking the teacher is exaggerating. “They think their child is perfectly well-behaved at home with civilized language — how could they possibly behave as the teacher describes at school?”

They will question the teacher’s competence, even think the school’s approach is somewhat outdated and behind the times, and this can easily escalate into a direct complaint.

With this constant risk of being complained about, many teachers who originally wanted to seriously discipline their children now waver endlessly between “actively disciplining” and “just giving up.”

“Conflicts between family and school are increasing. Parents complain over the smallest things, favor their own children, and the consequences of complaints ultimately fall on frontline teachers — which makes teachers tiptoe around managing students, and students become even more lacking in character and rule awareness.” Muyue sums up. There’s a saying that rings true: “What basic education teachers endure will eventually radiate into every industry.”

“When I feel it’s really hard and whatever I do is wrong, I also tell myself: forget it, less trouble is better than more, just turn a blind eye.”

Guiyi says, “But the moment I stand at the podium and look at those thirteen and fourteen-year-old kids below, whose every opening word is profanity and trash memes, my instinct as a teacher makes it truly hard to just turn a blind eye. The internal struggle is agonizing.”

This oscillation between giving up and unwillingness has become the norm for many young frontline teachers in Shenzhen.

In the future, with fewer students to go around, each child will seem even more precious at home. The internet updates even faster, and more trash memes keep stuffing themselves into children’s brains.

Shenzhen teachers look at this trash-meme-filled break time before them and can do little more than sigh. Just as Guiyi keeps comforting herself in her heart:

“Teachers are human too.”
